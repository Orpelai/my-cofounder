# Problem

Conventional plans list initiatives but hide user anchors, dependencies, maturity differences, and movement. This causes bespoke work on commodities, premature outsourcing of novel advantages, and brittle roadmaps.

# Trigger diagnostic

- Is the user need explicit and observable?
- Can the team name the components required to meet it?
- Are dependencies and failure points visible?
- Are components treated differently by evolution stage?
- Is build/buy/partner based on landscape evidence rather than preference?

Use the map when at least two answers are “no.”

# Required inputs

- Named user, need, and value-delivery context.
- Current value chain and major dependencies.
- Evidence on component ubiquity, certainty, market availability, and change.
- Cost, capability, supplier, lock-in, and regulatory constraints.
- Decision horizon and strategic options.

# Roles

- **Map owner:** frames the user need and maintains the artifact.
- **Domain contributors:** validate components and dependencies.
- **Commercial/operations owner:** validates suppliers, costs, and constraints.
- **Challenger:** tests position, movement, and inertia assumptions.
- **Decider:** owns resulting strategic choices.

# Procedure with decision gates

1. Name one user and the need whose satisfaction anchors the map.
2. **Gate 1 — valid anchor:** stop if the “need” is an internal output or technology preference.
3. List visible user-facing capabilities, then decompose their necessary dependencies.
4. Draw dependency links from user need through the value chain.
5. Remove components that do not materially enable the anchored need.
6. Position each component on evolution: **genesis, custom-built, product, commodity/utility**.
7. Record evidence and confidence for every position; ranges are acceptable.
8. **Gate 2 — map integrity:** require domain review of critical dependencies and low-confidence positions.
9. Mark movement: what is becoming more common, understood, standardized, or utility-like?
10. Identify inertia, switching cost, supplier power, data constraints, and regulatory exposure.
11. Generate strategic options: build, buy, partner, outsource, open, standardize, differentiate, or wait.
12. Match operating practice to component context rather than imposing one method everywhere.
13. **Gate 3 — coherent action:** reject plays inconsistent with evolution, capability, economics, or risk.
14. Run “what-if” scenarios for supplier failure, commoditization, demand shifts, and competitor moves.
15. Select actions with owners, leading signals, decision dates, and reversibility.
16. Re-map when evidence changes; compare maps rather than silently moving components.

# Output contract

- Dated map with user anchor, dependencies, and evolution positions.
- Evidence/confidence register for critical components.
- Movement, inertia, and exposure annotations.
- Build-buy-partner decision table.
- Strategic action sequence with assumptions and review triggers.
- Archived prior version for learning.

# Metrics

## Outcome

- Decision-specific result: lead time, cost, resilience, adoption, or strategic differentiation.
- Avoided custom-build cost or improved performance of a differentiating component.

## Leading

- Critical components with sourced positions and owners.
- High-risk dependencies tested or mitigated.
- Build-buy-partner decisions closed by deadline.

## Guardrail

- Vendor concentration, switching cost, security, privacy, reliability, and margin.
- Investment in unproven genesis components beyond approved risk budget.

# Failure modes

- Drawing an architecture diagram without an evolution axis.
- Starting from technology instead of user need.
- Treating evolution as a quality score or roadmap schedule.
- Assuming every component naturally moves at the same speed.
- Using exact coordinates to imply false precision.
- Copying generic strategic “doctrine” without context.

# Guardrails

- Label disputed positions and preserve dissent.
- Verify vendor availability, economics, and lock-in before sourcing decisions.
- Keep security, privacy, and regulatory data outside untrusted maps.
- Do not outsource a claimed differentiator without testing strategic consequences.
- Use the map to generate and challenge options, not to automate commitment.

# Adaptation notes

Wardley Mapping is an originator/practitioner technique. It improves shared situational reasoning but does not establish causal advantage or forecast evolution precisely.

For a small business, map only the 10–20 components that affect the active decision. Pair the map with customer evidence, supplier quotes, and a financial model.
