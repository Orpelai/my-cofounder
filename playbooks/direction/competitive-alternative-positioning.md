# Problem

Buyers interpret unfamiliar products through alternatives and market categories. Starting with a slogan or blank positioning statement hides the dependencies among differentiation, value, segment, and context.

# Trigger diagnostic

- What would the buyer use or do if this offer did not exist?
- Which capabilities are uniquely stronger than those alternatives?
- What customer outcome follows from each difference?
- Who cares urgently and measurably about that value?
- Which market frame makes the value obvious without creating false expectations?

Proceed only when answers can be tested with real buyers.

# Required inputs

- Recent win/loss, customer, and prospect interviews.
- Sales notes identifying status quo and no-decision.
- Product capability evidence and competitor/alternative verification.
- Segment behavior, urgency, willingness-to-pay, and usage data.
- Current messaging, conversion, deal-cycle, retention, and objection data.

# Roles

- **Positioning owner:** founder or product-marketing lead.
- **Customer evidence owner:** interviews buyers and lost prospects.
- **Product owner:** verifies capability claims.
- **Commercial owner:** validates objections and deal behavior.
- **Challenger:** detects invented uniqueness and category wishful thinking.

# Procedure with decision gates

1. Select one offer and one buying context.
2. List **competitive alternatives** buyers would choose without the offer, including “do nothing.”
3. Rank alternatives by observed frequency, not internal prominence.
4. **Gate 1 — buyer reality:** stop if alternatives come only from competitor lists.
5. Compare capabilities against each important alternative.
6. Keep only capabilities that are demonstrably different and relevant.
7. Translate each differentiated capability into customer value: “which outcome changes, for whom, and how?”
8. Quantify value where possible using time, risk, revenue, cost, or quality.
9. **Gate 2 — defensible value:** remove differences buyers do not recognize or that cannot be substantiated.
10. Identify customers who care most about the value because of their circumstances, constraints, or current alternative.
11. Define best-fit characteristics and explicit poor-fit conditions.
12. Choose a **market category/context** that makes the differentiated value easy to understand.
13. Test alternative frames for clarity, relevance, expectation, and willingness to continue.
14. **Gate 3 — context fit:** reject frames that create the wrong competitors or product expectations.
15. Build a positioning brief and derive messaging; do not collapse positioning into one tagline.
16. Test in sales calls, landing pages, and onboarding with predeclared metrics.
17. Review win/loss and retention evidence; revise the linked components together.

# Output contract

- Ranked competitive-alternative table with evidence.
- Differentiated capability → customer value chain.
- Best-fit and poor-fit customer characteristics.
- Selected market category plus rejected frames and rationale.
- Positioning brief, proof points, and claim limitations.
- Message test plan with decision thresholds and review date.

# Metrics

## Outcome

- Qualified conversion, win rate, retained revenue, and reduced no-decision among best-fit opportunities.

## Leading

- Buyers who accurately restate the differentiated value.
- Best-fit share of qualified pipeline.
- Proof-point usage and objection resolution.

## Guardrail

- Refunds, churn, sales-cycle length, discounting, and expectation mismatch.
- Unsupported comparative claims or brand-trust incidents.

# Failure modes

- Starting with desired category or tagline.
- Defining competition as only similar products.
- Calling features unique without comparison evidence.
- Confusing a benefit with measurable customer value.
- Targeting a demographic rather than customers who urgently care.
- Repositioning from a few enthusiastic comments without behavior.

# Guardrails

- Use verified buyer language; preserve contradictory evidence.
- Substantiate comparative, financial, and performance claims.
- Do not misrepresent category membership or competitor capabilities.
- Separate positioning choices by materially different segment or use case.
- Treat message tests as experiments, not proof of product-market fit.

# Adaptation notes

Dunford’s method is an originator-authored practitioner framework. Use it as an operating hypothesis and validate with win/loss behavior, conversion, sales-cycle, retention, and willingness to pay.

For a new venture with few customers, interview users of the current alternative and run sales conversations before finalizing the category.
