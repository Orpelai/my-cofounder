# Problem

Teams often copy the industry’s factors of competition. The result is convergent offers, rising cost, and incremental differentiation buyers barely notice.

# Trigger diagnostic

- Can buyers distinguish the offer without seeing the logo?
- Do competitors invest in nearly the same factors at similar levels?
- Are roadmap items mainly parity requests?
- Are expensive features weakly used or valued?
- Are noncustomers rejecting the category for recurring reasons?

Proceed when the current value curve is convergent and real alternatives exist.

# Required inputs

- Defined buyer or noncustomer group and use context.
- Direct evidence about buying criteria, alternatives, rejection, and willingness to switch.
- Competitor and alternative-offer data.
- Cost-to-serve by major offering factor.
- Current price, margin, adoption, retention, and usage data.

# Roles

- **Decider:** business owner.
- **Facilitator:** strategy lead.
- **Evidence owner:** customer researcher.
- **Economics owner:** finance/operations.
- **Challenger:** checks table stakes, safety, and feasibility.

# Procedure with decision gates

1. Define the specific market boundary, buyer, and job; do not map “everyone.”
2. List 6–12 factors on which the industry competes and invests.
3. Score the offering level buyers receive for the company and major alternatives.
4. Draw the **as-is strategy canvas**: factors horizontally, offering levels vertically.
5. **Gate 1 — valid baseline:** stop if scores are opinions without buyer or market evidence.
6. Mark similarity, overinvestment, underinvestment, and cost-heavy factors.
7. Review alternatives and noncustomers, not only direct competitors and current customers.
8. Ask the four official questions:
9. **Eliminate:** which taken-for-granted factors should disappear?
10. **Reduce:** which should fall well below industry standard?
11. **Raise:** which should rise well above industry standard?
12. **Create:** which factors never offered by the industry should appear?
13. Quantify expected buyer value and cost effect for every proposed move.
14. **Gate 2 — value innovation:** require both a credible buyer-value leap and sustainable economics.
15. Draw the **to-be value curve** and state its focused strategic theme.
16. Test comprehension, preference, switching intent, and willingness to commit with target buyers.
17. **Gate 3 — behavioral evidence:** revise if buyers do not recognize or act on the difference.
18. Pilot the smallest coherent version; measure value, cost, and guardrails.
19. Decide to scale, iterate, or stop; preserve the as-is baseline for comparison.

# Output contract

- As-is and to-be strategy canvases with dated evidence.
- Completed ERRC grid with rationale and cost/value estimates.
- Buyer and noncustomer evidence log.
- Strategic theme and offer hypothesis.
- Pilot scope, owner, budget, metrics, and decision date.
- Explicit table stakes and protected constraints.

# Metrics

## Outcome

- Conversion/switching versus the prior offer, contribution margin, and retained customer value.

## Leading

- Target buyers who identify the difference and make commitments, pilots, or preorders.
- Cost removed through eliminate/reduce choices.

## Guardrail

- Retention, refunds, service quality, safety, compliance, and adoption by affected valuable customers.

# Failure modes

- Choosing factors from internal feature lists instead of buyer language.
- Scoring competitors from reputation rather than evidence.
- Raising and creating without eliminating or reducing cost.
- Mistaking novelty for value.
- Ignoring nonconsumption and noncustomers.

# Guardrails

- Never eliminate legal, safety, accessibility, or trust requirements.
- Treat all scores as relative hypotheses with dates and sources.
- Preserve buyer heterogeneity; create separate canvases for materially different segments.
- Do not publish competitor claims that have not been verified.
- Require unit-economics viability before scale.

# Adaptation notes

Blue Ocean Strategy is an originator/vendor framework. Its canvas and ERRC grid organize choices; they do not prove that a “blue ocean” exists or that a move will win.

For small ventures, use rough but sourced 1–5 scores, one customer segment, and one-week concept tests. Replace scores as behavioral and economic evidence arrives.
