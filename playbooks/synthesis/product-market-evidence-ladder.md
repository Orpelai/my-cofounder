# Problem
Teams often treat interviews, signups, payments, usage, and retention as interchangeable
proof. They answer different questions. This ladder makes the evidence claim-specific,
raises the cost of evidence only as the decision warrants, and prevents weak signals
from masquerading as validated demand.

# Trigger diagnostic
- What exact customer, problem, value, channel, or retention claim is being tested?
- What decision will change if the claim is supported or rejected?
- Is current evidence stated behavior, observed behavior, payment, or repeated value?
- What is the cheapest ethical observation that can discriminate between hypotheses?
- What evidence level is proportionate to the cost and reversibility of the decision?

# Required inputs
- One falsifiable claim and competing explanations.
- Defined segment, user, payer, and buying context.
- Current evidence with provenance and dates.
- Decision threshold, deadline, and maximum test cost.
- Instrumentation or manual observation method.
- Privacy, consent, and customer-harm constraints.

# Roles
- **Claim owner:** defines the claim and decision.
- **Discovery lead:** gathers qualitative evidence without pitching.
- **Experiment owner:** runs behavioral or transactional tests.
- **Evidence reviewer:** challenges sampling, bias, and causal claims.
- **Decision owner:** funds, revises, or rejects the next commitment.

# Procedure with decision gates

1. Write the claim, alternative explanation, and decision it informs.
2. Place current evidence on the ladder:
   - Level 0: founder assumption.
   - Level 1: repeated interview pattern about real past behavior.
   - Level 2: observed behavior or costly customer action.
   - Level 3: commitment or payment under realistic terms.
   - Level 4: repeated use, retention, renewal, or referral.
   - Level 5: controlled evidence that the intervention caused the outcome.
3. Record evidence quality, sample limits, contradictions, and freshness.
4. Select the next level necessary for the decision—not automatically the highest.

**Gate 1 — claim clarity:** do not test until the segment, behavior, threshold, and
decision consequence are explicit.

5. Design the smallest test that can move the claim up, down, or split it.
6. Predeclare success, harm, futility, and time limits.
7. Collect evidence without changing definitions after results appear.
8. Review disconfirming cases and alternative explanations.

**Gate 2 — commitment:** irreversible investment requires behavioral evidence at a
level proportionate to downside; interview enthusiasm alone is insufficient.

9. Decide: reject, revise, hold, advance, or scale the claim.
10. Record what changed and the next evidence requirement.
11. Run an after-action review when the result contradicts a major belief.

# Output contract
- Claim card with segment, decision, and competing explanation.
- Evidence ledger with level, source, date, and confidence.
- Test contract and ethical guardrails.
- Result, contradictions, and decision.
- Next evidence level or explicit stop.

# Metrics
- Claims with explicit evidence levels.
- Time and cost to resolve a material uncertainty.
- Conversion between ladder levels.
- Payment, activation, retention, renewal, and referral where relevant.
- Forecast calibration of claim confidence.
- Invalidated assumptions before major investment.

# Failure modes
- Counting opinions as behavior.
- Mixing users, buyers, and segments.
- Advancing the ladder on one vivid customer.
- Moving success thresholds after seeing results.
- Treating payment as proof of retention.
- Running controlled experiments before instrumentation is trustworthy.

# Guardrails
- Obtain appropriate consent and minimize customer data.
- Never fabricate scarcity, commitments, or payment evidence.
- Preserve negative and contradictory observations.
- Do not use the ladder as a universal maturity score for the company.
- Match evidence burden to risk, reversibility, and affected stakeholders.

# Adaptation notes

Early solo ventures may progress through manual sales and delivery. Mature products may
start with strong behavioral logs but still need qualitative explanation. The ladder
organizes evidence strength; it does not guarantee product-market fit.
